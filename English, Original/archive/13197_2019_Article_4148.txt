The inﬂuence of thermal hydrolysis process on emulsifying
properties of potato protein isolate
Emilia Drozłowska1•Marta Weronis1•Artur Bartkowiak1
Revised: 28 February 2019 / Accepted: 3 November 2019 / Published online: 11 November 2019
/C211Association of Food Scientists & Technologists (India) 2019
Abstract The article considers the inﬂuence of the thermal
hydrolysis process on emulsifying properties and viscosity
of potato protein isolates. During the study, there wasdemonstrated a signiﬁcant improvement in emulsifying
properties of the protein isolates as a result of denaturation.
Thermal hydrolysis process includes high temperature andpressure. The improvement in emulsion stability main-
tained in some variants up to a week has been observed.
Signiﬁcant correlations were found between emulsiﬁcationactivity and emulsion stability after 1 week ( p[0.05).
The rheological measurement showed an increase in vis-
cosity after HPP. As the proposed method of the proteinisolate modiﬁcation does not affect the chemical compo-
sition of the protein solution, but only its molecular
structure, it enables to increase the emulsifying propertiesin a simple and cost-effective way.

Introduction
The aim of protein modiﬁcation is to obtain products
containing complete protein, improvement of their func-
tional properties and reduction in the level of anti-nutritive
and harmful substances. Protein formulations are acquiredeither from plant or animal raw materials. They are subject
to modiﬁcations by chemical, physical and enzymatic
methods resulting in changes in the composition or size ofmacromolecules.
Physical methods include for example heating pro-
cesses. The usage of a temperature of 60 /C176C–90/C176C and a
proper heating time inﬂuence the functional properties of
the protein such as water absorption, emulsiﬁcation and
solubility abilities (Ustunol 2014 ). This is the most com-
mon way in the industry of improving the properties of
proteins. Extrusion is another physical method combines
the impact of water, pressure, heat and mechanical forces.As a result of the decomposition of protein chains, the
subunits form new intermolecular hydrogen, peptide and
disulﬁde bonds. The protein subjected to this process ischaracterized by much better viscosity and the ability to gel
and foam stabilization (Samaranayaka and Li-Chan 2011 ).
The high temperature treatments lead to protein denatura-tion and increase the rate of some chemical reactions in the
product. Moderate protein heating at 60–90 /C176C may affect
the effectiveness of the protein as an emulsiﬁer if the
induced conformational change can increase its
hydrophobicity, without a decrease in solubility (Mied-zianka et al. 2012 ).
Protein isolates are products with the contest of proteins
up to 90–92% by weight (Xia et al. 2016 ). They are used in
the food industry as, for example, ingredients of pickling
brine and meat products. They may be also substituted for
milk protein in beverages, in vegetarian products andnutrients for infants and children. They are also used to
improve the nutritional value of products for example soya
or pea protein isolate in products for vegan. Potato proteinis added to products because it is lower allergenic and has
well amino acid balance.

Potato proteins are divided into three fractions: patatin (up
to 40%), protease inhibitors ( *50%), and other high
molecular weight proteins ( *10%). Patatin is the major
protein from potato ( Solanum tuberosum ) consists of a group
of 40–42 kDa glycoproteins (Pihlanto 2017 ). There are
many forms of patatin, they can be divided into four groups:
A, B, C and D. All forms combine almost identical compo-sition of amino acids. Patatin is the main spare protein in the
potato and has an enzymatic activity that allows the digestion
of fats. The isoelectric point of patatin values between 4.5
and 5.2. Many researchers report about the role of this protein
in the potato defense mechanism (Ba ´rta and Ba ´rtova´2008 ;
David and Livney 2016 ; Wegener et al. 2014 ). Another
group of potato proteins is protease inhibitors, which con-
stitute from 20 to 30% of the potato juice extract. They aredivided into seven subs due to the molecular weight and
composition amino acids. The biological function has not
been described, but it is believed to be involved in plantdefense mechanisms. Thermal treatments of this protein at
pH 7 led to creating additional bands with molecular masses
between 100 and 150 kDa. Fractions with similar molecularmass were too visible after high pressure treatments Mech-
anism of denaturation patatin is depends of environment
temperature. In 45 /C176Ct o5 5 /C176Ca-helical contribution is
unfolding, whereas in 50 /C176Ct o9 0 /C176Cb-stranded are
destroying. After the cooling process some parts of protein
structure are refolded, it was observed especially for a-he-
lical (Ba ´rta and Ba ´rtova´2008 ). The solubility of patatin in
changing under pH of environment for example in pH 7 the
thermal modiﬁcation has no effect of solubility (Ralet andGue´guen 2000 ).
The aim of the work was to investigate the inﬂuence of
thermal hydrolysis on the emulsifying properties of pota-toes proteins.
Materials and methods
Preparing trials and thermal modiﬁcation
Potato protein isolate was purchased from Solanic (Poland)
and the rapeseed oil from ZT Kruszwica (Poland). Sampleswere an aqueous solution of 600 g with a concentration of
10% protein isolate. The protein solution was then poured
into two beakers and heat treated with stirring for 15 and30 min respectively at 100 /C176C using a magnetic stirrer
(IKA, Germany) at 4000 rpm for 5 min. After that both
samples boiled for 30 and 15 min were divided into two100 ml samples each and subjected to thermal hydrolysis
with autoclave Prestige Medical Classic (Prestige Medical,
France) for 60 and 120 min at 126 /C176C and pressure of
1.5 bar. Finally, the following variants of processes were
summarized in Table 1.The control sample in subsequent studies was a 10%
protein solution prepared using a magnetic stirrer with
RCT Basic safety control (IKA, Germany) at 4000 rpm for
5 min at a temperature in the range from 50 to 60 /C176C.
Rheological measurements
For initial solutions as well as those subjected to heat
treatment and thermal hydrolysis were conducted rheo-
logical measurements at 20 /C176C and presented as viscosity
changes in the function of shear rate. The measurements
were conducted using the AR G2 rheometer (TA Instru-ments Ltd., USA) with a 60mm cone plate geometry.
Emulsion propertiesEmulsions were prepared with the ratio of protein solutions
0.5%, 1%, 2% and 3%. The amount of the added oil phasewas at the same level of 10% by weight of the solution. The
whole mixture was heated together with stirring with
magnetic stirrer RCT Basic safety control (IKA, Germany)until it reached 60 /C176C. After exceeding this temperature,
the appropriate amount of oil was added and the sample
was mixed for 5 min at a speed of 4000 rpm. The obtainedpre-emulsions were homogenized with the use of a Magic
LAB homogenizer with a UTC module (IKA, Germany)
for 5 min with a rotational speed of 12,000 rpm at roomtemperature of about 20 /C176C.

trials were centrifuged at 2000 rpm/min for 10 min at20/C176C. The supernatant was collected and protein content
was determined by microbiuret method (Itzhaki and Gill
1964 ). Calibration of the assay was performed with a
standard bovine serum albumin solution. The protein sol-

Results and discussion
Emulsifying activity and solubility index
The emulsiﬁcation activity was expressed as the ratio of the
cream phase layer to the total emulsion after centrifugation,
the same test used to evaluate the emulsiﬁcation activitywas used by Gannasin et al. ( 2012 ). The emulsiﬁcation
efﬁciency was analyzed for the 1%, 2% and 3% stabilized
variants of the protein and all variants of thermal modiﬁ-cation. At 1% and 2% of the protein additive, the emulsi-
ﬁcation efﬁciency increased with respect to the unmodiﬁed
sample along with the modiﬁcation of the modiﬁcationparameters. However, with the addition of 3%, perfor-
mance deteriorated by thermal hydrolysis. The best emul-
siﬁcation performance was described for the sample withthe addition of 2% protein and the variant IId, while the
weakest for 1% of the protein additive and the thermal
hydrolysis variant IIc.
As can be seen in the graph, the emulsiﬁcation efﬁ-
ciency of one of the protein dilution variants decreased
after the longest thermal hydrolysis process, however, thisis not the rule. Similar results have been described by Khanet al. ( 2015a ,b) for protein derived from sweet potatoes.
There is no correlation between the inﬂuence of thermal
hydrolysis and the efﬁciency of emulsifying speciﬁc vari-
ants in the cited. There were attempts to correlate EAvalues with solubility, but there was no signiﬁcant corre-
lation (Khan et al. 2015a ,b). Signiﬁcant correlations were
found between emulsiﬁcation activity and emulsion sta-bility after 1 week ( p[0.05). Samples containing low
level of EA in spite of initial high stability after 1 week
dropped below an established range for a stable emulsion.
The stability study led to the same conclusions as in the
cited work. The thermal hydrolysis process has a slightinﬂuence on the changes in solubility, and the correlation
between emulsiﬁcation yield and solubility is not statisti-
cally signiﬁcant. Variants IIc and IId improved the solu-bility of the isolate, but this result does not translate into
emulsiﬁcation efﬁciency and emulsion stability. The solu-
bility of all variants was quite similar. In no case is 100%soluble, since the isolate is a mixture of different fractions,
the solubility of which varies under the given conditions
(Fig. 1).

Many studies highlight the positive effect of heating
protein solutions on their emulsifying properties. However,
it is emphasized that both increased pressure and temper-
ature can increase these properties, but this does not alwaysaffect the long-term stability of the emulsion. According to
Galazek, Dickinson and Leward, this may even lower the
stability of emulsions in the case of a protein with a largenumber of 11 s units due to the increased dissociation ofsubunits and/or aggregation caused by the formation of
intermolecular disulﬁde bridges by combining –SH/–SS–
(Galazka et al. 2000 ).
For the protein subjected to the strongest hydrothermal
modiﬁcation IId the emulsion with ESI stability of 100%
after 1 week was obtained only at 3% of the added protein,
this is probably caused by the smaller diameter of the dropsin the emulsion, and the fact that the absorption layer of the
oil droplets also hangs on aggregated proteins (Nishinari
et al. 2014 ).

Rheological resultsViscosity tests of 10% potato protein solutions conﬁrmed
that the lowest viscosity was characteristic for the startingprotein without modiﬁcation. At a shear rate of 0.1 [1/s],
the solution viscosity of this protein was 0.2326 Pa s, while
at 1.0 [1/s] it was almost 10 9lower at 0.03142 Pa s.
Pseudoplastic ﬂuids have no ﬂow limit and are thinned
with shear, which means that their apparent viscosity
decreases with increasing shear rate. After the heat treat-ment process, the viscosity of this protein solution
increased in comparison to the control sample, the viscosity
of the solution at a shear rate of 0.1 [1/s] was almost 17times higher and at a shear rate of 1.0 [1/s] close to 8- fold
higher. The thermal hydrolysis process led to increase the
viscosity of this protein in each analyzed process variant.At a shear rate of 0.1 [1/s], the solution viscosity of this
protein for variant IIa and IIc was 337.9 Pa s and1253 Pa s respectively, while at a shear rate of 1 [1/s], the
solution viscosity of variant IIa and variant IIc the viscosity
was 57, 82 Pa s and 152.6 Pa s respectively.
Viscosity measurements of 10 % potato protein solutions
after 30 min of thermal modiﬁca tions conﬁrmed that the lowest
viscosity was observed for star ting protein solution without any
further modiﬁcation. At a shear rate of 0.1 [1/s], the viscosity ofsuch protein solution was 0.2326 Pa s, whereas at 1.0 [1/s] it
was 0.03142 Pa s. This conﬁrms t hat such protein solution is a
Non-Newtonian liquid of pseud oplastic character because
apparent viscosity constantly decreased with increased shear
rate. The longer the shear stress works, the greater thedestruction of the structure. The observed effect is the result of
the tendency of protein molecule s to orient their major axis in
the direction of ﬂow, this is common in the case of high proteinconcentration in the solution like in research (Damodaran
2017 ). This phenomenon is too described for a whey protein
(Zhang et al. 2006 ) After the heat treatment process, the vis-
cosity of such solution increased as compared to the control—it
was 80-fold higher at a shear rate of 0.1 [1/s], and 30 fold higher
at the shear rate of 1.0 [1/s]. The thermal hydrolysis hasincreased the viscosity of protein solution for each variant of the
analysed process. At a shear rate of 0.1 [1/s], the viscosities of
solutions for variant IIb and II d were respectively 143.3 Pa s
and 1079 Pa s. At a shear rate of 1 [1/s], the viscosity of this
protein solution for variant IIa and IIc was respectively
30.43 Pa s and 135.7 Pa s.
During the studies the solution of potato protein isolate
was treated with high pressure 1.5 bar at 120 /C176C in lab-
scale autoclave, which led to the improvement of emulsi-fying properties. Khan and co-workers drew the same
conclusion (Khan et al. 2014 ). During the research pressure
was of the order of 0.1 MPa, whereas in the publication the
maximum applied pressure was 600 MPa, it showed that
the adopted method of improving the emulsifying proper-
ties is correct. All tested solutions are characterized by amonotonic decrease in stress as the shear rate increases and
therefore they were classiﬁed as thixotropic liquids (Dzi-
ubin´ski and Kiljan ´ski2002 ). Such character of the potato
protein solutions was also indicated by Holm and Eriksen,
who investigated the emulsifying properties of the con-
centrated potato protein concentrate (Holm and Eriksen1980 ). Modiﬁcation with HPP is increasing hydrophobicity
and the formation of patatin complexes with a mass
between 100 and 150 kDa (Baier and Knorr 2015 ).
The literature often is analyzed the effect of pressure on
the same emulsion, but not protein isolates. In similar work
relating to the modiﬁcation of potato protein, the effect ofHPP on the rheological properties of the emulsion. A pseu-
doplastic character was also noticed, which was explained as
the effect of dissociation of protein aggregates by breakingweak bonds during shear increase from 1 to 100 [1/s] (Khan
et al. 2015a ,b). This phenomenon may occur in the emulsionJ Food Sci Technol (March 2020) 57(3):1131–1137 1135

non-spherical particles may be aligned with the ﬂow ﬁeldand/or the ﬂocs may be distorted and disrupted (McClements
