Adapting political economy 
analysis for malaria programmingTECHNICAL BRIEF
Background
Good policies do not always result in good development 
outcomes. This is because policies are not operationalised 
in a vacuum, so the context in which they are adopted 
and implemented has a bearing on their success. 
Conducting a political economy analysis (PEA) helps to 
understand how change happens in a particular setting. 
By situating policies within that environment, we can 
more easily identify and mitigate the factors that influence 
implementation. 
Donor agencies and development organisations are 
increasingly using PEA as they move away from a one-
size-fits-all approach. PEA uses a qualitative approach to 
identify the contextual factors — political, economic, 
social and cultural — that drive change, influence 
decision-making and shape power relations. The findings 
of the PEA are then used to adapt programmes, increasing 
their effectiveness. Because PEA identifies issues that are context specific, 
findings are not transferrable to other settings. However, 
a methodological framework is a helpful tool for other 
practitioners to adapt to their geography or sector — 
once a PEA has been adapted for a specific context, it can 
periodically be used to assess the situation and update 
the findings to inform future programming. Political 
economy is fluid as it is shaped by institutions and actors. 
Any shift in this environment, for example a change in 
leadership, alters the context for implementation, making 
it easier or more difficult. PEA should not be a static tool 
but used continually to assess the factors that are driving 
or impeding change. Figure 1 demonstrates how the PEA 
approach is used continually to learn from, and adapt to, 
the changing context.

Why political economy analysis 
for malaria programming
The World Health Organization’s (WHO) Global Malaria 
Programme provides recommendations and guidelines for 
the control and elimination of malaria. These policies have 
been successfully implemented in a variety of countries 
since 2000, resulting in 12 countries certified as malaria free 
and 23 achieving zero-malaria cases for three consecutive 
years.[1] Yet, there are many countries where malaria 
remains a significant public health concern and policies 
have not been successfully adopted or implemented.[1]
Malaria is a complex disease and a variety of factors 
determine the success of an intervention. Even when there 
is strong policy adherence, successful outcomes can be 
affected by external factors that impact on transmission, 
for example climate fluctuations. For areas where a 
lack of progress cannot be explained by ecological or 
technological reasons, it is helpful to explore the political 
economy of a setting. Political will to implement a policy 
is often affected by governance, incentives and power 
dynamics.
PEA for malaria programming is used to understand the 
actors that operate in the health sector, how they interact with each other, which institutions are responsible for 
resource allocation and mobilisation, and the motivations 
and ideologies of those in positions of influence. 
Understanding these structures and relationships helps to 
identify bottlenecks and barriers preventing coordination. 
We can then use these findings to improve effectiveness 
through adaptations to existing programmes, and to 
inform the design of new ones.
Method
Malaria Consortium developed a PEA framework to 
identify key assumptions and factors with the potential to 
affect the implementation of malaria policies. The findings 
of the PEA will help future national malaria programmes 
and their partners to navigate the political landscape, 
promoting more effective programme implementation. 
The framework draws elements from the former 
Department for International Development's Drivers of 
Change,[2] Frameworks for Understanding the Political 
Economy of Sectors[3] and the Three Layers of Problem-
Driven Governance and Political Economy Analysis[4] as

Limitations
Qualitative interviews conducted face-to-face are time 
intensive and costly, especially if they require travelling 
large distances to interview stakeholders at national and 
subnational levels. While virtual interviews can mitigate 
some of these costs, this comes at the expense of the 
depth required for PEA. The topics discussed during PEA are sensitive in nature and face-to-face interviews are 
recommended to allow the interviewer to build rapport 
with interviewees. The type of information produced 
through a PEA is also specific to the setting being studied, 
which hinders cross-country comparison.
Adapting a political economy approach for Nigeria: The case of SuNMaP2
Background
Despite ambitions laid out in Nigeria’s National Malaria 
Strategic Plan 2014–2020 to reach pre-elimination and 
reduce deaths from malaria to zero by 2020, progress to 
reduce the disease has been limited.[7]. Malaria remains a 
leading public health challenge in Nigeria and, according 
to estimates in the most recent World Malaria Report, 
in 2020, the country accounted for 27 percent of global 
malaria cases and 32 percent of deaths attributable 
to malaria.[1] To accelerate Nigeria’s progress towards 
malaria elimination, it is essential to understand how the 
political economy environment affects the successful 
implementation of policies that can move the country 
towards its goals. 
Overview and objectives
In 2020, we adapted a PEA approach for the Support to 
the National Malaria Programme in Nigeria 2 (SuNMaP2) 
and National Malaria Elimination Programme (NMEP) to 
gain a better understanding of government stewardship, 
the prioritisation and allocation of public funding, 
decision-making processes in the country’s health systems 
and the role of the private sector. The project sought to 
understand how the power, interests, incentives, values, 
beliefs and ideologies of different individuals and groups 
— at national and subnational levels — shape the context 
for implementation of the malaria elimination programme 
at federal level and in SuNMaP2-supported states.
The PEA aimed to:
• determine the root causes of challenges within the 
heath sector
• identify key actors and institutions that contribute to 
health sector challenges 
• identify how key actors and institutions influence 
health sector decision-making
• understand how actors interact with each other, how 
information flows and how this impacts on decision-
making
• understand where there are gaps in malaria coverage 
and service delivery
• explore how key actors define or perceive the barriers 
that inhibit successful malaria programming. Methodology
The PEA followed the three-stage method presented 
above. We conducted the situational analysis through 
a desk review that gathered evidence on government 
stewardship, philanthropic and private sector engagement 
and fiscal space analysis as key drivers of the country’s 
health systems. We then carried out stakeholder mapping 
to establish the sample frame for interviews and FGDs. 
Interviews and FGDs took place with officials at national 
state and LGA levels including: political leaders (e.g. 
state governors), officials with budgeting and planning 
responsibilities (e.g. finance and budget office), health 
sector officials (e.g. the MoH and NMEP), legislature 
officials (e.g. chairs of influential committees), field/facility 
staff (e.g. research institutes and civil society organisations), 
partners and donor agencies, and the private sector. 
The results of the situational analysis informed the 
development of tools for primary data collection. We used 
semi-structured open questions to conduct interviews 
and FGDs and explored a number of themes including: 
• resource generation: how and by whom the health 
sector is funded
• resource allocation: how resources are shared among 
services, locations or communities
• regulation and legislation: the measures used to 
ensure the safety and quality of services; and how are 
they enforced
• performance monitoring and management: 
approaches to ensuring that services are delivered as 
planned in terms of quality and quantity
• human resources: decisions on the deployment of 
staff and how they are managed
• sourcing of inputs: how commodities are purchased 
and distributed 
• oversight and accountability: how resources are 
tracked and reported against planned priorities.
The PEA also assessed progress on capacity development, 
transition to the NMEP and State Malaria Elimination 
Programmes (SMEPs), and mentoring in relation to the 
sustainability of malaria within NMEP and SMEPs.
Findings
We analysed the data collected during the PEA in Nigeria 
to identify where opportunities and barriers to policy 
reform and sustainability exist; how the programme can 
promote positive change towards a sustainable national 
programme; and which approaches may be effective in 
giving malaria greater importance on the policy agenda. 
Our analysis found that governments do not prioritise 
investment in malaria because there is no expectation 
from citizens, and the incentives of reducing the disease 
are unclear. The government invests in areas that are 
politically attractive and where strong advocacy and 
communications exist. This high-level advocacy for 
malaria is missing due to a lack of malaria champions in 
Nigeria. Although the NMEP leadership is technically 
competent, it is limited by bureaucracy. The programme is 
not structurally well positioned — the NMEP has to seek 
approval from the Director of Public Health, Permanent 
Secretary and Minister of Health — which impedes 
communication and collaboration. This poor positioning 
is compounded by weak coordination between actors in 
the malaria and health sectors, where multiple partner 
organisations work in silos. International funding creates 
competition rather than collaboration, and partner organisations are accountable to funders rather than 
communities. Coordination between government 
departments, the private sector and civil society is also 
absent.
To encourage investment in malaria, high-quality 
data are needed to build evidence that demonstrates 
the link between health and economic performance, 
which requires integrating quality data from the public 
and private sector. This evidence can then be used to 
improve resource allocation and communicated to the 
government to demonstrate the incentives for investing in 
the malaria programme. 
Advocacy driven by citizens is also needed and will require 
the involvement of civil society and social behaviour 
change campaigns to challenge citizens’ acceptance of 
malaria. Integrating malaria into the health system would 
help to break down silos between actors, reduce reliance 
on donor funding and shift accountability mechanisms 
away from donors. The inclusion of the private sector 
is also needed to drive down the cost of commodities 
and fill gaps in service delivery. Further discussion of the 
findings and limitations of this study are presented in 
Political Economy Analysis for Malaria Programming in 
Nigeria project report.[8]

Recommendations for 
conducting a PEA
A number of conditions can affect the success of a PEA 
approach. We recommend the following considerations 
when employing this method:
• The information collected during a PEA is highly 
sensitive. Conduct PEA in close collaboration with 
relevant governing bodies to provide guidance on 
access, and ensure the findings are validated by 
key stakeholders. The success of the PEA in Nigeria 
is attributed to the participatory process, which 
involved the NMEP and Federal MoH. 
• The selection of key stakeholders for interviews and 
focus groups discussions is a critical stage in the PEA 
process. A power-interest matrix can be a useful tool 
to identify who these key stakeholders are. Great 
care should be taken to ensure that those who are 
interviewed have a good country knowledge and have 
the ability to represent an accurate understanding 
of the political economy landscape. The validity of 
the findings is fundamentally linked to this selection 
process. PEA revolves around certain dynamics that 
evolve over time. Conduct these analyses regularly 
to monitor changes in the context and maintain the 
relevance of the findings.Conclusions
Although intensive, PEA is a valuable tool when designed 
and conducted with these recommendations in mind. 
Data-informed decision-making and tailored approaches 
based on evidence have become central characteristics 
in international malaria guidance in recent years. It is 
important to recognise the value of including qualitative 
information to contextualise and adapt guidelines to 
the political economy of a country or subnational unit. 
Designing programmes using high-quality quantitative 
data and employing a well-designed PEA will accelerate 
progress towards international targets such as the Global 
Technical Strategy for Malaria 2016–2030 and the 
Sustainable Development Goals.
