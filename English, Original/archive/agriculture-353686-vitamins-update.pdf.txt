Abstract: A number of catastrophes could block the sun, including asteroid/come t 16 
impact, super volcanic eruption, and nuclear war with the burning of cities (nuclear 17 
winter). Previous work has analyzed alternate food supplies (e.g. mushrooms 18 
growing on dead trees, bacteria growing on natural gas). This was shown to be 19 
technically cap able of feeding everyone with macronutrients (protein, 20 
carbohydrates, and lipids) and minerals, although economics and politics remain 21 
uncertain. The present work analyses vitamin availability in such alternative food 22 
scenarios. The vitamin content of vari ous alternate foods is compared to the U.S. 23 
recommended daily allowance (RDA) as well as the average requirement defined 24 
by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) and insufficiencies of single food 25 
sources are discussed. Single alternate food sources ar e always deficient in some 26 
vitamins, and the problems associated with this are discussed.  It is clear that to 27 
prevent disease in an alternative food scenario a wide range of foods must be 28 
consumed or the diet must be supplemented. Backup plans discussed in clude 29 
chemical synthesis of vitamins, plants grown with artificial light and growing 30 
bacteria rich in certain vitamins. Finally, insights from this analysis are provided 31 
for combating existing micronutritional deficiencies using alternative foods today.  32

1. Introduction  37 
Agriculture and the global human civilization dependent on it relies  on access 38 
to sunlight. A number of global catastrophes could partially block the sun, which 39 
Agriculture 2018 , 8, x FOR PEER REVIEW  2 of 20 
 would result in significant reductions in agricultural output and the potential for 40 
mass human starvation. The three most probable sun -obscuring scenarios include:  41 
(1) bolide (asteroid/comet) impact, (2) a super volcanic eruption or continental basalt 42 
flows, or (3) and nuclear war with the burning of cities (nuclear winter) [1,2]. 43 
Although these are low probability events, they have finite non zero probabilities 44 
[3], with the most probable being nuclear war. Two estimates based on quantitative 45 
models indicate the chance of full -scale nuclear war is ∼1% per year [4,5]. This is 46 
significant as the majority of the nuclear powers possess more than the pragmatic 47 
limit of n uclear weapons (where the direct physical negative consequences of 48 
nuclear weapons use are counter to national interests) [6]. Even a modest release of 49 
nuclear weapons on target cities as a one -sided [6] or regional nuclear war [7,8] could 50 
create a nuclear  autumn that would starve millions throughout the world [6 -10]. In 51 
addition, there are other less -severe risks to the agricultural system. These include: 52 
(1) abrupt climate change [11], (2) super weed [12], (3) extirpating crop pathogen 53 
[13], (4) complete loss of pollinators [14], (5) super bacterium [15], or (6) super crop 54 
pest [16]. Prevention of these catastrophes would obviously be preferable, but 55 
despite the highest probability severe sun -blocking risk being under human control, 56 
at present global de -nuclearization appears unlikely in the short term and there are 57 
not reliable options for the other large risks.  58 
Therefore, a backup plan is desirable. Previous work has analyzed alternative 59 
food supplies that could be viable in these scenarios and optimistically found that the 60 
global human population could be maintained even in the most severe catastrophes 61 
(e.g. 5 years of blocked sunlight) converting wood and fossil fuels to food [3,17]. 62 
These c alculations were, however, based on macronutrients and although the 63 
number of kilocalories could be produced to feed everyone globally, micronutrients 64 
were not evaluated in detail. Micronutrients play a central part in metabolism and 65 
in the maintenance of human tissue function and are therefore critical for human 66 
health [18]. To provide a complete viable backup food supply for conventional 67 
agricultural collapse further research is required [19]. Previous work has been done 68 
on the basics of minerals [17] and  therefore this paper will focus on vitamins in order 69 
to begin to fill in these knowledge gaps. This preliminary study briefly summarizes 70 
the alternative food pathways and then evaluates the micronutrients (specifically 71 
vitamins) available to meet the perc ent of the U.S. recommended daily allowance 72 
(US RDA) as well as the average requirement defined by the European Food Safety 73 
Authority (EFSA) with these foods and closest analogous foods when data are not 74 
available. In some global catastrophes some human po pulations may only have 75 
access to a single alternate food. For these situations, the micronutrient profile for a 76 
person on a 2,100 kcal diet of each alternate food is evaluated for risk of disease. 77 
Ideally, populations would have access to an array of alte rnative foods. For these 78 
scenarios, a representative alternative food diet with adequate micronutrients is 79 
developed and analyzed. These results are discussed to develop a method to viably 80 
produce vitamins to fortify the entire human population for this su n blocking 81 
scenario and other agricultural catastrophic scenarios.  82

 2. Materials and Methods  83 
2.1. Alternative food routes and assumptions  84 
Although it is still possible to provide for the calories necessary for human 85 
existence in the worst scenarios even wi thout industry [20], in most cases these 86 
catastrophes would destroy industry only regionally. The majority of global 87 
industry would still function under these global catastrophic risk scenarios 88 
(particularly the six crop killing scenarios). If the sun were  not completely blocked, 89 
algae would grow in the ocean vigorously because the cooling climate would bring 90 
deep nutrient rich water to the surface [17]. Also, the algae would be protected from 91 
the high ultraviolet light levels produced especially by nuclear  winter [8] and 92 
therefore feed significant amounts of fish, which humans already rely on for a major 93 
source of food. Currently, 13.8% to 16.5% of the animal protein intake of the global 94 
human population are from fish, crustaceans and mollusks [21]. Moving to a more 95 
fish based diet is therefore the least radical form of alternative food.  96 
Solutions independent of the sun include those that can convert wood into food. 97 
This would include converting wood to food with mushrooms directly, but also 98 
indirectly wood softened with mushrooms or non -woody material could be 99 
converted into food via cellulose digesting beetles [22], rats [23] and ruminants (e.g. 100 
cows, sheep, goats, etc.) [24]. Mushrooms, cows, sheep and other ruminants are 101 
currently widely used as food sour ces, whereas beetles and rats are less commonly 102 
eaten. Another method to turn wood to food is to use chemical engineering. Current 103 
cellulosic biofuel systems transform cellulose into sugar and then feed the sugar to 104 
a fungus to create ethanol [25]. However , to produce human edible food this process 105 
could be stopped at sugar. If the pre -digestion of fiber were done very carefully, 106 
even a non -cellulose digester could be fed, such as chickens, which are already 107 
raised widely raised for human food.  108 
Another alt ernative food route, which is particularly important in the transition 109 
from conventional agriculture to alternative foods because of ramp rates [6], is 110 
taking leaves from woody and non -woody plants and making tea, chewing and 111 
spitting out the solids, or ma king leaf protein concentrate [26].  112 
A final set of routes is the use of fossil fuels to alternative foods. Mushrooms can 113 
be grown on coal similar to growing on peat [27]. In addition, natural gas digesting 114 
bacteria [28] could be fed to humans. These alter nate foods were shown to be 115 
technically capable of feeding everyone with macronutrients (protein, 116 
carbohydrates, and lipids) and minerals could be extracted from the ground [17].  117

2.3. Analysis  128 
For scenarios where a population would be limited to a single alternative food 129 
the micro nutrient profile for a person on a 2,100 kcal/day (8.79 MJ/day) diet of each 130 
alternate food is evaluated for risk of disease. This diet represents the calorie level 131 
average of males and females 19 -50 and the US RDA is set to meet or exceed the 132 
specific nut rient requirements to be sufficient for 97.5% of the population. This is 133 
thus treated as the conservative vitamin requirement. For scenarios where 134 
populations would have access to the full range of alternative foods a representative 135 
alternative food diet w ith adequate micronutrients is developed and analyzed. The 136 
feasibility of the different alternate food sources was estimated to construct an 137 
example diet and the calories from each alternative food is tabulated.  138 
Then a second type of micronutrient analysi s is presented. Rather than only rely 139 
on the values that represent the ideal food intake from the developed world (the 140 
percentages of US RDA) a second estimate is determined. This less conservative 141 
analysis is presented for the average requirement (AR), wh ich is defined by the 142 
European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) as the level of (nutrient) intake that is 143 
adequate for half of the people in a population group, given a normal distribution 144 
of requirements [29]. The percentage of the average requirement for a 3 0-39 year old 145 
female with a physical activity level (PAL) of 1.6 (moderately active) were used 146 
(corresponding to the same 8.7 MJ/day as shown in Table A2) [29].  It should be 147 
noted that nutrients outside of the US RDA were excluded, but omega -3 fatty acids  148 
could be provided by eating fish.  149

3.2. Diseases Caused by Inadequate Micronutrients from Single Alternative Food Diets  221 
The diseases associated with inadequate micronutrient intake because of 222 
consuming a single alternative food diet are reviewe d. In addition, insights from this 223 
analysis are provided for combating existing micronutritional deficiencies using 224 
alternative foods today.  225 
3.2.1. Vitamin A  226 
Only liver, chicken, milk, eggs and bacteria provide sufficient vitamin A if eaten 227 
alone accordin g to Figures 1 and 2. Vitamin A deficiency resultant from the other 228 
diets would first cause nyctalopia (night blindness) and then xerophthalmia, 229 
keratomalacia, and complete blindness can also occur as vitamin A has a major role 230 
in phototransduction. In add ition, low vitamin A diminishes the ability of people to 231 
fight infections, which would also increase mortality if adequate calories were 232 
present in a global catastrophe where it was not available. Vitamin A deficiency is 233 
shockingly common today, affecting 21.1% of pre -school children and 5.6% of 234 
pregnant women worldwide [30]. Of the non -standard alternative foods, 235 
consumption of bacteria directly or vitamin A supplements derived from them 236 
could help improve this global issue today.  237 

Agriculture 2018 , 8, x FOR PEER REVIEW  10 of 20 
 3.2.2. Vitamin B1 (Thiami ne) 238 
Only liver, oyster mushrooms, milk and bacteria provide adequate levels of B1 239 
Following Figures 1 and 2. Severe vitamin B1 deficiency occurs as beriberi in the 240 
tropics and is distinguished from the less severe degree of deprivation occurring in 241 
tempera te climates [31]. The former affects the cardiovascular system resulting in a 242 
fast heart rate, shortness of breath, and leg swelling, while the latter affects the 243 
nervous system resulting in numbness of the hands and feet, confusion, and pain 244 
[31]. Even in  the developed world alcohol abuse, dialysis and taking high doses of 245 
diuretics increases the risk of developing B1 deficiency [31]. The growth and 246 
supplementation of the diet with oyster mushrooms or bacteria could be uncommon 247 
alterative foods that could help this condition today. It is interesting to note that the 248 
type of mushroom grown determines its micronutrient level and significantly more 249 
research is thus needed to determine if growing a wide range of mushroom in an 250 
alternative food scenario may be o f assistance in micronutrient availability.  251 
3.2.3. Vitamin B2 (Riboflavin)  252 
Tuna, liver, chicken, both mushrooms, milk, eggs and bacteria all provide 253 
enough vitamin B2 as can be seen in Figure 1 and Figure 2. The consequences of 254 
vitamin B2 deficiency can le ad to weakness and fatigue and can influence drug and 255 
lipid metabolism [32].  256 
3.2.4. Vitamin B3 (Niacin)  257 
Figures 1 and 2 show that beef, milk, eggs, tea, and dandelion do not provide 258 
enough B3 in a single alternative food diet. B3 is essential in all cells  for energy 259 
production, metabolism and DNA repair. Severe deficiency of vitamin B3 results in 260 
pellagra, which results in primarily dermatitis, diarrhea, dementia and eventually 261 
death [33]. Pellagra is common developing regions as well as among those living  in 262 
poverty, those who are homeless, refugees and other displaced people due to their 263 
dependence on food aid. Current B3 deficiencies can be solved with alternative food 264 
growth of mushrooms or bacteria.  265 
3.2.5. Vitamin B5 (Pantothenic Acid)  266 
Tuna, chicken, e ggs, and both mushroom varieties are the only single source 267 
alternative foods providing enough vitamin B5 according to Figures 1 and 2. 268 
Vitamin B5 deficiencies are rare and normally result in fatigue and other symptoms.  269 
3.2.6. Vitamin B6  270 
As can be seen in Figures 1 and 2, only bacteria, tea and dandelion single -source 271 
alternative foods do not provide enough B6. Vitamin B6 deficiency causes microcytic 272 
anemia, electroencephalographic abnormalities, dermatitis with cheilosis (scaling on 273 
the lips and cracks at the corners of the mouth), depression and confusion, and 274 
weakened immune functioning [34,35].  275

 3.2.7. Vitamin B9 (Folate)  276 
Only liver, both mushrooms, and eggs provide enough vitamin B9 when on only 277 
a single alternative food diet. Deficiency in vitamin B9 [ 36] causes megaloblastic 278 
anemia, a condition in which the bone marrow produces oversized immature red 279 
blood cells. In pregnant women, lack of vitamin B9 can cause severe or even fatal 280 
birth defects.  281 
3.2.8. Vitamin B12 (Cobalamin)  282 
Only mushrooms, bacteria, tea and dandelions did not have sufficient B12 by 283 
either standard, while all of the meat -based sources of alternative foods provided 284 
the necessary B12 even on a single source diet. Vitamin B12 deficiency is a common 285 
cause of macrocytic anemia and has been implicated in a spectrum of 286 
neuropsychiatric disorders [37].  287 
3.2.9. Vitamin C (Ascorbic acid)  288 
Only the tea and dandelions have adequate vitamin C. Without vitamin C, 289 
scurvy occurs after 3 months and is normally due to lack of consumption fresh fruits 290 
and vegetables [38]. Initially those suffering from scurvy have weakness, feel tired, 291 
and have sore extremities, then decreased red blood cells, gum disease, and bleeding 292 
from the skin occur, poor wound healing, personality changes, and finally death 293 
from infection or bleeding [39,40]. A remarkable 6 -8% of the current global 294 
population is suffering from scurvy and this can be higher in regions suffering from 295 
extreme poverty [41]. Public education about the potential to cure the disease even 296 
without access t o common fruits and vegetables either by eating what is often 297 
considered weeds (dandelions) or making teas from common plant leaves could 298 
reduce scurvy today.  299 
3.2.1.0. Vitamin D  300 
Only liver, both types of mushrooms, eggs and bacteria provided the US RDA 301 
of vitamin D on a single alternative food diet. Vitamin D deficiency causes rickets (a 302 
condition that results in soft bones in children, which result in bowed legs, stunted 303 
growth, and bone pain [42]). It can also cause growth retardation, skeletal 304 
deformitie s, and increase risks of fractures. For adults not enough vitamin D results 305 
in osteopenia and osteoporosis, osteomalacia and muscle weakness [43]. Again the 306 
use of bacteria and mushrooms could reduce current vitamin D deficiencies.  307 
3.2.1.1. Vitamin E  308 
Only  eggs and bacteria provide enough vitamin E on a single alternative food 309 
diet as shown in Figure 1. Vitamin E is important for normal neurological function 310 
[44]. Vitamin E deficiency can cause significant health issues [44,45].  311

4. Discussion  346 
There is good evidence of having complete access to micronutrients (vitamins + 347 
minerals) to benefit patients suffering from critical illnesses [18]. Most benefits from 348 
micronutrients appear to come from a well -balanced diet [18], which is what should 349 
be th e goal of each community in an alternative -food scenario. The minerals could 350 
largely be mined and have been investigated previously. Though this mix of 351 
alternative foods shown in the sample diet shown in Table 1 and 2 would provide 352 
adequate vitamins, not e very person may have access to this mix. In particular, 353 
poorer people would only have access to the lower cost alternative foods. Based on 354 
current prices, these would likely be bacteria, enzymatic sugar, fish, and leaf extract 355 
[52]. In addition, vitamin re quirements could be different for different people at 356 
various stages of their lives. Therefore, it is useful to have additional ways of 357 
providing vitamins. One method would be removing vitamins from certain alternate 358 
foods to use as supplements for those p eople who do not eat those particular 359 
alternate foods. For instance, if many people do not want to eat significant amounts 360 
of bacteria (and the other major nutrient sources shown in Figure 1 are not available), 361 
the vitamin E could be removed from the metha ne-digesting bacteria and fed as a 362 
supplement. Another potentially low -cost source of vitamins would be bacteria that 363 
can grow on fiber. A higher cost source of vitamins would be bacteria that can grow 364 
on food that is digestible by humans.  365 
Humanity has al ready established methods to synthesize some vitamins [53]. 366 
This could potentially be expanded in catastrophic scenarios which block the sun, 367 
but retain industry, and potentially form a multivitamin pill. However, this would 368 
not be feasible in scenarios wh ere industry is disabled: electricity could be disrupted 369 
by a solar storm, high -altitude electromagnetic pulses from nuclear detonations, or 370 
a super computer virus [54,55]. Non -industry scenarios would generally still have 371 
sunlight, so farming nearly any c rop by hand would be feasible. However, it may be 372 
that high calorie per hectare crops need to be favored, which could have less than 373 
optimal nutrition.  374 
Agriculture 2018 , 8, x FOR PEER REVIEW  14 of 20 
 One other option for vitamins would be growing plants with artificial light. 375 
However, this is very ineff icient and therefore expensive, so it should only be used 376 
as a last resort [56]. And again, this would not be feasible without industry and 377 
electricity.  378 
The most extreme scenario is losing industry and the sun. This could occur if the 379 
sun is blocked and if  international cooperation breaks down. Alternatively, if there 380 
is high solar dependent renewable energy penetration (photovoltaics, concentrating 381 
solar power, wind power, hydropower, and biofuels), a loss of the sun could mean 382 
a collapse of industry as we ll in the short term. The benefits of making the transition 383 
to renewable energy anyway is that large stores of fossil fuel assets would be 384 
preserved and could be tapped over time to both provide for energy needs in an 385 
extreme sunlight -blocking catastrophe as well as act as a source of alternative foods. 386 
Finally, full -scale nuclear war could be coupled with multiple high altitude 387 
electromagnetic pulses (HEMPs). In these scenarios, alternate foods would be 388 
required, but industrial synthesis of vitamins would not be possible. Therefore, 389 
other techniques such as growing bacteria rich in certain vitamins may be required.  390 
It is clear, however, from this study that far more work needs to be done in this 391 
area. First, much better nutritional information is needed ab out the alternative foods 392 
themselves, which are less common (e.g. extracting tea from leaves of different 393 
species). Thus, future work is needed to quantify the vitamin content of some of the 394 
actual alternative foods (instead of proxies). In addition, detai led nutritional profiles 395 
of all the variants of different alternative foods must be analyzed carefully for 396 
strategic application (e.g. for a region capable of only producing fish, what is the 397 
ideal mushroom types needed to compliment the fish nutrient prof ile?). In addition, 398 
a more granular analysis is needed for the available specific alternative foods in a 399 
given area (e.g. what type of fish is available in a given region and what is its 400 
nutritional profile). Similarly, the nutritional information (both ma cro and micro) of 401 
secondary sources of alterative foods must be quantified (e.g. the nutritional 402 
makeup of chicken is well known, however, how does that change if chicken is 403 
grown on only bacterial -pre-digested wood?). This information would allow for the 404 
analysis of a bare minimum of alternative food diversity that would prevent major 405 
diseases like scurvy. In addition, the nutritional requirements of a low -calorie diet 406 
should also be looked at carefully in the cases where human activity would reduce 407 
if ina dequate calories were available from any of the alternative food pathways in a 408 
particular region. Once an alternate food diet is estimated to be safe with analytical 409 
methods, trials on animals and eventually humans would provide final validation.  410 
In the Hy ogo Framework for Action [57], this study supports preparedness and 411 
identifying risks. Previous work has shown that investing in interventions using 412 
alternative foods is cost effective in both the U.S. [58] and globally [59]. However, in 413 
order to be prepar ed, these solutions for food catastrophes must be distributed, so 414 
this is a gap in the Post 2015 Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction in that this area 415 
is not adequately addressed. Training for these type of catastrophes could be 416 
integrated into existing training efforts. In the scenarios that are the focus of this 417 
Agriculture 2018 , 8, x FOR PEER REVIEW  15 of 20 
 work (sun being blocked) or lesser agricultural tragedies, industry will still generally 418 
be functioning, and so will cities. Future work could be quantifying vitamins for the 419 
scenarios of losing  industry and losing industry and the sun (and also vitamins for 420 
animals in all the scenarios). Ensuring that everyone could have adequate nutrition 421 
would reduce the chance of civilization collapsing, from which humanity might not 422 
recover. This reduced cha nce would benefit the far future, which has been -argued 423 
to have overwhelming importance [60]. Finally, malnutrition and hunger -related 424 
disease, results in about 6 million preventable deaths per year in children under 5 425 
years old currently [61]. This proble m when the global agricultural system is 426 
functioning is trivial compared to any of the catastrophic situations when it is not. 427 
Although research in alternative foods could help feed today’s starving, the fact 428 
there are still millions of hungry people when there is adequate food shows that the 429 
economics and politics of feeding people both now and in a catastrophe are also 430 
important future projects.  431 
5. Conclusions  432 
If the sun is blocked by a large asteroid or comet, super volcanic eruption, or 433 
nuclear war, alt ernate foods would be required. Single alternate food sources are 434 
always deficient in some vitamins, and the problems associated with this are 435 
discussed. However, the analysis shows that a diversity of these foods could provide 436 
adequate vitamins. Processin g might be required to aid digestibility of these 437 
vitamins. If this diversity is not feasible for all people, alternate methods could be 438 
used such as growing bacteria rich in certain vitamins and industrial synthesis. In 439 
the cases of losing industry, peopl e would likely have adequate nutrition. In the case 440 
of losing the sun and industry, people would likely require growing bacteria rich in 441 
certain vitamins.  442
