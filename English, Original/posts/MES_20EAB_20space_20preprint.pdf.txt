 1 Introduction 30 The cost of providing food in space is excessively [1] high [2]. There is a need to develop a sustainable and 31 affordable food growing solution for environments in which it is difficult to grow food. Such environments include 32 the Earth’s orbit, the Moon, or Mars, and even on the Earth in a sun-obscuring global catastrophe, such as a 33 supervolcanic eruption or a nuclear war causing cities to burn and the smoke inducing a nuclear winter. Existing space 34 food technologies are expensive, not scalable, and not suitable for long duration missions. This article investigates the 35 use of electroactive bacteria (EAB) as a resilient food production source for personnel in space. The goal was to 36 characterize the potential of a food production method from CO2 and compare it to relevant technologies, in order to 37 mitigate certain restrictions of a manned space mission. 38 A number of challenges make agriculture in space a difficult premise, including lack of gravity, ionizing 39 radiation, limited on-board space, and contaminants [3]. There is a limit to greenhouse efficiency due to the 40 infrastructure required to protect from cosmic radiation. Current space foods are prepackaged food and fresh food 41 shipped to space after being grown on the Earth [4], neither of which are sustainable for long term space missions. 42 One alternative growing method to conventional agriculture is growing crops with artificial light, which is not 43 economically feasible for feeding a large population even on the Earth [5]. In addition, artificial light infrastructure 44 consumes large amounts of electrical energy, since phototrophic organisms (such as crop plants and microbes) have 45 low light to chemical energy conversion efficiency [6]. Since the dominant cost of a space mission is fuel [7], 46 minimizing the elements needed for food production in space is essential for reducing costs. Other potential solutions 47 not including plants of algae are foods from bacteria, which provide direct sources of nutrients needed by human 48 beings. EAB can release or retrieve electrons from solid state electron donors or acceptors using extracellular electron 49 transfer (EET) mechanisms. This allows them to use a wide range of electron donors/acceptors meaning that they can 50 survive in different environments [8]. There are several genera of EAB that grow under a variety of conditions on 51 Earth, from moderate to extreme environments, making them an ideal candidate for being grown in space. EAB are 52 also found naturally and widely spread across microbial communities, from wastewater systems to the human 53 gastrointestinal tract. Auto-lithotrophs, including some EAB, do not require organic molecules (such as sugar) or 54 valuable oxygen in order to grow. As far as we know, their environmental adaptability could give them added benefit 55 as a direct source of food if they are found to be edible like many other bacteria. 56 Bioelectrochemical systems (BES) are emergent technologies that convert organic and inorganic material 57 into electricity and/or chemical products through reduction and oxidation reactions [9]. BES can be divided based on 58 their applications; microbial fuel cells for the oxidation of organic/inorganic matter to the production of electricity, 59 and microbial electrosynthesis (MES) and microbial electrolysis cells, which reverse the microbial fuel cell process, 60 to product synthesis by applying an electric current [10]. BES results in a wide range of applications spanning from 61 energy production, waste recovery, bioremediation to food/nutrient production. The recent advancements of BES are 62 promising for assessing their use in manned space missions for the generation of electricity from human wastes and 63 the use of electricity for the synthesis of useful chemicals [11,12], moreover contributing to biologically sustained life 64 support. Such an adaptable design would allow the manned space missions to cut down the mass of shipped products 65 
 
 as they would be synthesized on board by MES. The use of MES in space for food and nutrient production has not 66 been explored yet. The advantages of a bioelectrochemical system in space are apparent by their efficiency when 67 compared to photosynthesis, the supply of electricity compared to energy from light, and the electrode surface area to 68 microbial cell surface area [13]. A study investigated the utilization of MES to produce food on the Earth to obviate 69 the use of additional stored food and artificial light photosynthesis during a global catastrophe [14]. While MES is 70 currently not a competitive food alternative for the Earth during a global catastrophe, the high caloric efficiencies of 71 this system may significantly reduce the equivalent system mass (ESM) for space food production. Thus, MES could 72 compete with conventional space food, prepackaged food, and be more energy efficient than a competing alternative 73 space food, microalgae SCP, specifically Spirulina platensis. Spirulina platensis [15] was selected to be grown in 74 space using artificial light photosynthesis because it is among the most productive photosynthetic microorganisms. 75 This study is similar to [1] which investigated the use of hydrogen-oxidizing bacteria (HOB) single cell protein (SCP) 76 as food in space. The current study assesses the economic feasibility of using MES with a mixed microbial community 77 in space for acetic acid (AA) production. AA is a product of fermentation; when diluted 4-6% by volume, is vinegar.  78 On the Earth, AA is commercially used in the manufacture of vitamins, antibiotics, hormones, organic chemicals, and 79 as a food additive [16]. 80 EAB have an advantage over other organisms because of their ability to utilize electrons from the electrode 81 surface on which they grow and transfer those electrons across their biofilm via extracellular electron transfer (EET). 82 A diagram of the MES process [14] is depicted in Figure 1, for which the overall chemical reaction is described by 83 Equation 1, based on partial reactions from literature [17]. Since fuel is the dominant cost of a space mission, and 84 fuel consumption is directly proportional to payload mass, the economic feasibility of MES in space was addressed 85 by using an ESM technique [18], established by the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA).  86  87 2 Methods 88 The ESM results were used to select between competing subsystems. In this study, the ESM of MES is 89 calculated and compared to the ESM of prepackaged food, microalgae SCP, and HOB SCP, calculated in a previous 90 study [1]. An International Space Station (ISS) mission, a Moon mission, and a Mars mission were assessed, each 91 consisting of 5 crew members (CM) for a duration of 3 years. Currently, more EAB research is needed to make 92 sufficiently accurate assumptions [19], but sufficient information exists to achieve the goals of this work, which is to 93 make a comparison to relevant space food technologies to inform prioritization of developments in the field. Values 94 need to be consistent with each other to evaluate the technology for the purpose of food production. MES-AA is treated 95 as the sole calorie source for this study to form an equitable comparison to other food production methods. In addition, 96 this food production method can be used as a resilient food for sun-blocking global catastrophic risk (GCR) scenarios 97 in accordance with its ability to use feedstocks not directly dependent on agriculture and sunlight, such as water and 98 industrial or atmospheric CO2 [14]. Both CO2 capture [20] and utilization [21] are rapidly expanding areas of research, 99 containing value in sustainable chemical energy production and energy integration. The design for the MES system is based on the most promising experimental lab-scale 114 research ([23]). Figure 1 illustrates a flow diagram of the MES food production concept which includes all major 115 equipment, excluding storage tanks. Liquid reaction medium and CO2 from the spacecraft’s air purification system 116 are mixed prior to their entry to the cathode and fixed at 32 oC. The cathode compartment includes the EAB, electrodes, 117 and membranes needed for the MES reaction to occur. The reaction medium in both cathode and anode compartments 118 contains trace elements and synthetic nutrient solution ([23]). The anolyte composition is optimized with decreased 119 pH to favor proton crossing through the membrane. The inoculum used for EAB is considered to be a mixed microbial 120 

 
 consortium enriched with bacterial populations such as Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes and Actinobacteria 121 [24–26]. Experimental evidence suggests the use of aggregated forms of cells as a potential way to improve the 122 survivability of a microbial population in high-radiation microgravity environments and allow biofilms to thrive [27]. 123 The EAB biofilm could be grown, optimized, and stabilized prior to the launch of the spacecraft to aid its adaptation 124 in space conditions. The reaction starts by applying a specific potential, -0.85 V vs standard hydrogen electrode (SHE), 125 to achieve AA production. The liquid mixture is then moved to an EAB separator (biomass filter) where any remaining 126 EAB are filtered and collected to be recycled to the cathode compartment, followed by product separation through an 127 absorption column. Any byproduct production such as O2 and H2O can be recycled within the MES system or used 128 for the needs of the spacecraft.   129  130 2.2 Equivalent system mass 131 ESM equates all components of a subsystem with a unit mass for analog comparison using mass 132 equivalencies, derived by the NASA Life Support Baseline Values and Assumptions Document (BVAD) [28]. Before this production method is 225 sufficiently advanced for the proposed application, there are several technical challenges relating to its reliability as a 226 food production method. The appropriate cathode material should be identified to enhance the electrocatalytic activity 227 and microbial cell adherence to the cathode. The appropriate bacterial strain or community should be identified 228 specific to its food production employment, i.e. for AA production; perhaps genetically modified to improve 229 production rates. Condition monitoring should be performed to optimize energy consumption, biofilm formation time, 230 biofilm stability, and byproduct production rates.  Studies performed on microorganisms in microgravity confirmed 231 the increased production of viable cells, biofilm biomass, and thickness compared to normal gravity [38]. These 232 promising results are an advantage to BES since a healthier biofilm formation will drive better EET and therefore 233 higher efficiency rates (synthesis). Additionally, the effects of spaceflight on the physiology of EAB, specifically 234 Shewanella oneidensis manganese-reducing strain (MR-1), have been examined during transfer to the ISS [11]. 235 Potential impacts of a space environment have been thought to be an increase in cellular stress, which could negatively 236 

 
 impact growth performance, and a decrease in biofilm development, which could negatively impact growth and 237 byproduct formation. However, studies including electroactive cultures must be performed under space conditions to 238 verify this behavior. Field experiments in microgravity conditions with an MES food production system are required 239 to establish feasibility for manned space missions. 240  241 4.1 Alternatives comparison 242 This analysis indicates that the ESM of MES is lower than prepackaged food and microalgae (Figure 3). The 243 ESM of HOB remains the lowest alternative, and therefore lower cost compared to each food system. The mean ESM 244 of MES is 2.84x lower than microalgae, 1.38x lower than prepackaged food, and 1.86x higher than HOB SCP. The 245 apparent mass is the dominant contribution to the total ESM of MES for each mission; whereas on-board volume is 246 relatively insignificant for ISS and Moon missions (Figure 4). ESM results are higher in value for the Moon mission 247 than for Mars (Figures 3-4) due to location factors. This is because the Moon and Mars missions have different 248 shuttles, propulsion types, and transportation history (i.e. whether payloads are jettisoned during travel). ESM is not 249 the exclusive metric for a comparison study since it lacks considering reliability, safety, and palatability [39]. 250 However, it is pivotal for evaluating cost. The location factor is multiplied as the endcap of the analysis to magnify 251 the propulsion requirement. As MES productivity advances, MES-AA would become more competitive as a food 252 source in space. As the technology of MES advances and volumetric productivity and faradaic efficiency are 253 improved, the apparent mass of an MES setup will reduce and could come closer to (or optimistically pass lower than) 254 the ESM of HOB. Based on current, long-term sustained literature values for MES (volumetric productivity of 6.75 255 kg/m3medium/d and faradaic efficiency of 90%) the ESM of HOB is more favorable than for MES. In order to make 256 MES competitive with HOB in terms of ESM (for the expected faradaic efficiency of 70%), volumetric productivity 257 equal to 25 kg/m3medium/d or higher would be needed. High volumetric productivities have been achieved [40]; 258 however, they were not used in this analysis because it is not the exclusive key parameter; this system had much lower 259 titers and overall lower energy efficiency. Another advantage to the system used as a basis in this study is that it was 260 tested in long-term operation (hundreds of days). The electricity-to-calorie efficiency for MES-AA was calculated as 261 19.8% which is more favorable than the two competing microorganisms. The same conversion is 4.4% efficient for 262 microalgae SCP and 17.7% efficient for HOB SCP [1]. It is worth mentioning that the range of 50-90% faradaic 263 efficiency translates to 15.2-20.8% overall electricity-to-calorie efficiency, but the average expected value of 70% 264 faradaic efficiency corresponds to the 19.8% electricity-to-calorie efficiency value. 265  266 4.2 Life support considerations 267 Food is a category of life support and can be supplied via MES using resource recycling. Life support 268 subsystems are currently in place to improve air and water recovery and waste recycling but have low effectiveness 269 [41]. Table 4 compares each component of MES to prepackaged food and microalgae; of which the microorganisms 270 
 
 display higher effectiveness with resource recovery and recycling. Ammonia, providing the nitrogen for EAB, can be 271 recycled from urine. Water, consumed in electrolysis and microbial growth, can be supplied by the water production 272 in astronauts’ metabolism (mostly urine, perspiration, and exhalation); oxygen as a product of electrolysis can be used 273 for astronauts to breathe. The carbon in CO2 produced by the astronauts’ metabolism is consumed by microbial growth 274 and MES. Net MES activity (food production) can be stable with complete carbon recycling; meaning less expected 275 amounts of raw materials would be needed at the beginning of a mission leaving the Earth. These would include 276 specific nutrients for the bacterial culture, and the amounts required are small enough to be negligible in terms of mass 277 at launch. Resources such as CO2 and water may also be available from mission locations, particularly on Mars. The 278 prepackaged food option additionally requires operation of a Sabatier reactor that combines CO2 produced by 279 astronauts with H2 to yield methane waste and water; oxygen is recycled via electrolysis of the water product [42]. 280 The methane and excess water wastes add mass to the system and must be ejected. Although MES contributes to 281 biologically sustained life support, the pressure and atmospheric conditions would still need to be controlled. In 282 comparison with the current prepackaged food standard, the MES-based life support system substitutes for the 283 Environmental Control and Life Support System (ECLSS) equipment, but both systems would incorporate an identical 284 set of equipment (Sabatier, etc) as a backup. Thus, the backup does not need to be accounted for in either option, but 285 the ECLSS system needs to be accounted for as an ESM contribution of the prepackaged food option. Frozen EAB 286 starter cultures may be carried in the spacecraft for increased resilience of the MES life support system, but this was 287 not accounted for in terms of mass. 288  289   290

  292 4.3 Nutrient diversity and future work 293 This study explored MES-AA as the exclusive calorie source. A proper nutrition plan may consist of 294 significant portions of MES-AA but since this source does not provide all of the nutritional needs of a human, it would 295 need to be complimented by food sources that provide vitamins, minerals, fats, carbohydrates, and proteins. These 296 needs may be met in combination with other alternative foods such as prepackaged food, microalgae, HOB SCP [1] 297 or non-biologically synthesized carbohydrates from CO2 (García Martínez et al. to be published). The latter is the last 298 work on the current series of publications on alternative foods for space missions and contains a more holistic 299 comparison of the five food options mentioned above. Further research can be derived from this, moreover, combining 300 low-cost alternative foods for a mission. MES has been studied as an alternative food production method to feed 301 humanity during a global catastrophe inhibiting conventional agriculture, constituting a global catastrophic risk [14]. 302 Such a catastrophe could potentially provoke or lead to existential risk, threatening the survival or future potential of 303 humanity. 304 Alternative foods inherently produce food at lower relative cost or energy consumption than the most 305 apparent solutions: stored food and crops grown with artificial light photosynthesis. This is analogous to prepackaged 306 food and artificial light-grown microalgae. Refuges have been considered for reducing existential risk to humanity by 307 increasing the likelihood of recovery from a global catastrophe or for self-sufficiency [43]. The small scale MES food 308 solution may relate to a refuge or small community on Earth during a sun-inhibiting global catastrophe, similar to a 309 refuge in space. Furthermore, this may apply to off-grid communities, such as those in rural Alaska [44], in which the 310 cost of energy and product delivery is very high (analogous to current prepackaged food delivery to the ISS). 311 An additional option for food production from BES could be consumption of the EAB as a form of SCP, 312 similar to the HOB SCP produced from H2 gas described in the first article of this series [1]. This option was ultimately 313 not developed here since producing SCP from EAB as a space food would likely be worse in terms of ESM than SCP 314 from HOB. It is unlikely that a different electron donor (namely cathode electricity rather than H2) would increase the 315 metabolic rate of CO2 fixation. The reason is that the same metabolic pathways are used once the electrons get into 316 the cells [45], which means the cell growth rate will almost certainly be lower than that of HOB using external H2. 317 This would result in a lower production rate and thus higher ESM.  318 Researching the cultivation of EAB for SCP production and its composition could be useful on the grounds 319 of nutrient diversity. Even though EAB SCP has higher ESM than HOB SCP, it could still be useful in combination 320 if it could provide useful nutrients not found in the HOB SCP. However, no nutritional analysis of EAB SCP can be 321 found currently in literature. Bacteria in general have high protein content (30–60% of dry weight) and some have 322 high lipid content (50-70% of total biomass dry weight) [46]. In addition, different types of bacteria contain vastly 323 different micronutrient profiles, potentially including essential fatty acids [46], while generally having a high average 324 
 
 calorific content (over 5 kcal/g dry) [47]. Producing a variety of microbial foods could contribute to efficiently 325 achieving a balanced diet in space missions. 326 Even though the use of MES seems promising, further research needs to be performed to ensure the ability 327 and efficiency for food production in space. The survivability of EAB in space will directly affect the operation of the 328 BES and therefore the production rates. To the best of our knowledge, no studies have been performed to test the long-329 term survivability of EAB in space during BES operation, with the longest one lasting only 4 days [11]. However, 330 results from non-EAB studies showed that certain bacteria not only can endure extreme environments and survive in 331 space conditions but also demonstrated improved growth and activity rates [27,48]. Pseudomonas aeruginosa has 332 shown that spaceflight promotes biofilm formation and increases the numbers of viable cells, biomass, and thickness 333 of biofilms [38]. Although these studies showed promising results, cellular stress, fluid, proton exchange membrane 334 longevity, and gas distribution and separation must be studied prior to the application of BES in space [49]. 335 Furthermore, a food production reactor based on MES is yet to be developed but nevertheless space food production 336 is an additional potential application for this technology. Laboratory scale experiments consisting of a targeted species 337 of EAB have been difficult to manage [50]; however, a mixed culture of acetogenic EAB is more robust to 338 contamination by non-electrochemical microorganisms. Although the MES method discussed here is potentially 339 viable, this study is based on a theoretical process that has yet to be performed at this scale [29]; therefore, key 340 performance values may vary. 341  342 5 Conclusions 343  This analysis determined that MES-AA is a cost-reducing food substitution in space compared to 344 conventional prepackaged food and using artificial light photosynthesis to grow microalgae, which is among the most 345 productive photosynthetic organisms. Food produced via MES-AA would be at least one order of magnitude less cost 346 than growing conventional crops in space with artificial light, and significantly more energy efficient. The conversion 347 efficiency of electricity to caloric energy for MES-AA was calculated to be 19.8%. Fulfilling the caloric needs of a 5-348 crew mission, this would draw 3.45 kW from the system, all of which would eventually need to be rejected as heat. 349 In view of this favorable conversion efficiency, the electrical and thermal demands on MES-AA is lower than every 350 alternative food system considered in this study. However, the apparent mass is still relatively high due to low values 351 of MES volumetric productivity, which could potentially be improved via further research in MES for AA production. 352 For the latter reason, the ESM of HOB SCP, which leads the competition of food in space, is 1.86x lower than MES. 353 However, the final ESM of MES is on average 1.42x lower than prepackaged food and 2.84x lower than microalgae 354 SCP, and therefore the cost is proportionally lower. Although MES in space (and on the Earth) has significantly lower 355 cost and energy consumption than these alternatives, it must be complemented with other foods. This MES food 356 solution contributes to a biologically sustainable life support system by recycling CO2 into useful (edible) compounds 357 and recycling human contaminants, conducive to closed systems for manned space missions. 358
