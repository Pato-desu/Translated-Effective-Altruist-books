Last nontrivial update: 2019 May 20.SummaryLivestock grazing takes up 26% of the Earth's ice-free land area, making it one of thebiggest impacts that humans have on wild-animal suffering. Several studies suggest thatgrazing reduces wild-insect populations (and hence, probably wild-insect suffering) onpasture fields, but some studies point in the opposite direction. Cattle poop supportslarge numbers of insects, which adds uncertainty to the overall analysis. The fields oforganic cattle farms generally have higher insect densities than those of conventionalcattle farms.Whether cattle grazing increases or decreases total invertebrate populations on a givenpiece of land depends in part on whether the pasture is irrigated. For example, convertingformerly dry land in the Western USA into green pastures would likely increase totalinvertebrate populations.Note: This piece is one of a series of articles on the wild-animal impact of cattle grazing,and not all relevant considerations are discussed here.ContentsSummaryA note on terminologyIntroductionImpacts on insect populations in temperate climatesStudies finding that grazing reduced insect numbersCole, 1946Fielding and Brusven, 1995Rambo and Faeth, 1999Kruess and Tscharntke, 2002

Send in a wave of cattle to crop thosegrasses and we've diverted that ecological productivity to our own ends,depriving the local wildlife of food and habitat.However, if we think wild animals on the whole endure more suffering than happiness inlight of high infant-mortalityrates and short lifespans priorto painful deaths, then thisargument operates in reverse,favoring cattle grazing.However, there remains animportant empirical question:Does cattle grazing in factreduce wild-animalpopulations? How does this compare with ~5 million springtail-years prevented per bovine? I think thezooplankton mortality is somewhat less significant for a few reasons:A springtail-year of suffering contains several springtail deaths because mostspringtails (especially early-dying offspring) live much less than a year. 5 millionspringtail-years might be more like, say, ~50 million springtail deaths, or somethingin that ballpark.The zooplankton killed by irrigation would have died in some other way later on, sothe total harm done by killing them now is less than it seems. In contrast, bringing aspringtail into existence creates a full extra death (and suffering during life) thatwouldn't have otherwise existed.In regions where extensive irrigation of feed crops and pastures occurs, zooplanktonmortality might be several times more than the above number and might begin to rivalspringtail the deaths in importance. Of course, in dry areas requiring significant irrigation,cattle grazing seems like it might increase invertebrate suffering anyway just becauseirrigation creates more total vegetation on formerly dry land. So both zooplankton killingand increasing plant growth both argue against irrigation-intensive beef production.What if we count rotifers in addition to crustacean zooplankton? They could increaseinvertebrate deaths due to water withdrawal by perhaps an order of magnitude. However,if we count rotifers in addition to aquatic crustaceans, we should arguably also countterrestrial nematodes in addition to springtails, because nematodes and rotifers havesimilar numbers of neurons (Tomasik "Brain ..."). My general sense is that rotifers typicallyoutnumber crustacean zooplankton in lakes and rivers by about an order of magnitude, ormaybe two orders of magnitude; for example, see the "Snow (1974)" table in Tomasik("Zooplankton Densities in Lakes and Reservoirs") or the "Orsi and Mecum (1986)" and"Thorp and Mantovani (2005)" results in Tomasik ("Zooplankton Densities in Rivers"). Incontrast, the "Brady (1974)" and "Curl and Truelove (1986)" results in Tomasik("Abundances ...") suggest that soil nematodes outnumber springtails by perhaps two orthree orders of magnitude. So plausibly soil nematodes outnumber springtails more thanrotifers outnumber crustacean zooplankton. Hence, cattle grazing's effects on terrestrialinvertebrates still seem plausibly more important than its killing of aquatic invertebrates.Nonetheless, the zooplankton carnage involved in beef production isn't negligible, andgiven the high uncertainty in this analysis, the zooplankton-killing consideration deservesfurther scrutiny.
What about the fact that it's not clear if cattle grazing actually does reduce rather thanincrease populations of terrestrial invertebrates? Well, it's also not clear if withdrawingsurface water increases rather than decreases the total suffering of freshwaterzooplankton. Assuming the uncertainty on these two issues is of a similar magnitude,then downgrading our numerical estimates to account for this uncertainty over thedirection of the impact should apply similarly to both the springtail numbers and thezooplankton numbers.
