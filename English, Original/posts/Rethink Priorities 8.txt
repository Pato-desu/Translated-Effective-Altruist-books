Apr. 19, 2023, 2023 •Written By Aisling LeowBetter weather forecasting:Agricultural and non-agricultural benefits in low- andlower-middle-income countriesEditorial noteThis report is a “shallow” investigation, as described here, and wascommissioned by Open Philanthropy and produced by Rethink Priorities. OpenPhilanthropy does not necessarily endorse our conclusions.The primary focus of the report was to investigate whether improving weatherforecasting could have beneﬁts for agriculture in low- and lower-middle incomecountries, and evaluate how cost-eﬀective this might be. Note that this means wedid not evaluate improvements in weather forecasting against other potentialinterventions to achieve the same aims, such as the development of climate-resilient crops.We reviewed the academic and gray literature, and also spoke to seven experts.

In our report, we provide a brief description of weather forecasting and theglobal industry, before evaluating which farmers might most beneﬁt fromimproved forecasts. We then explore how predictions are currently made incountries of interest, and how accurate they are. We evaluate the cost-eﬀectiveness of one intervention that was o#en mentioned by experts, andhighlight other potential opportunities for grantmaking and further research.We don’t intend this report to be Rethink Priorities’ ﬁnal word on this topic andwe have tried to ﬂag major sources of uncertainty in the report. We are open torevising our views as more information is uncovered.Key takeawaysWeather forecasting consists of three stages. Large-scale atmospheric patterns — such as jet streams, oroscillations like El Niño — are signiﬁcant determinants of the weather.10
According to de Andrade et al. (2021) and Stacey et al. (2019), the key large-scalepatterns for subseasonal and seasonal weather in South Asia and sub-SaharanAfrica are the El Niño Southern Oscillation, Madden-Julian Oscillation, andIndian Ocean Dipole. We remove Ukraine and North Korea, as these areout of scope.
To calculate the number of smallholder farms per country, we primarily rely onthe estimates provided by Our World in Data (OWID; Ritchie & Roser, 2021). Thisinformation is not available for all countries, and so we identiﬁed major missingcountries based on agricultural GDP, by referring to a compilation sourced fromthe CIA World Factbook (Statistics Times, 2018). Seven LMICs with totalagricultural GDP >$10 billion were identiﬁed as needing separate estimates.For each missing country, we selected a comparison country that has data in theOWID source. Comparison country selection was not done in a systematic way,but was broadly based on similarity in terms of the relative importance ofagriculture (as measured by agricultural GDP as percent of total GDP) as well ascountry size. Our estimates assume that the number of smallholder farms andagricultural GDP are directly correlated, such that 1.5x agricultural GDP meant1.5x smallholder farm numbers, across all GDP levels. The choice of comparisoncountry does aﬀect our total signiﬁcantly, and as such we calculate twice to givean upper and lower bound.To account for the age of the OWID estimates, which are primarily from the1990s and early 2000s, we crudely adjust using the rate of growth in India overthe last two decades.Including only those countries included in OWID and crudely adjusting to the2020s gives a total of 216 million farms under 2 ha. The additional sevencountries add 43 million-90 million farms to the total, depending on themethod used.This is a rough estimate with a number of strong assumptions, including a singlegrowth applied to all countries on the basis of one comparison for India.As a test, we sense-checked the numbers for Nigeria, a major country that wasnot included in the OWID source. Our estimate using the method describedabove ranges from 6 million to 32 million, depending on the comparisoncountry. Sense-checks described below suggest that 32 million is slightly high foran upper bound, but overall, the estimates are within the range we have usedabove.
Sense-check 1: Nigeria has a population of 211 million (World Bank, n.d.-b).Multiple sources report that ~70% of the Nigerian population engages inagriculture (FAO, n.d.; Kamer, 2022). Another source suggests that 80% ofNigeria’s farmers are smallholder farmers, though the size of a smallholder farmis not deﬁned (Oyaniran, 2020). If we take these together, then we would expectthere to be 211 million × 0.7 × 0.8 = 118.2 million smallholder farmers in Nigeria.Assuming ﬁve household members per farm, this suggests 23.6 million farms.Sense-check 2: Nigeria has 69 million hectares of agricultural land (Sasu, 2022).Lowder et al. (2016) suggest that smallholder farms (<2 ha) operate on only 5% ofthe land, which would equate to a total of 3.45 million hectares. This would seemto suggest fewer than 10 million smallholder farms in Nigeria; for example, if wemade the strong assumption of an average size of 0.5 ha, the total number offarms would be 6.9 million.To calculate estimates assuming that a smallholder farm is deﬁned as “up to 5ha,” or “up to 10 ha,” we relied on the unadjusted OWID data. In each case, wecrudely constructed a ratio versus <2 ha farms. Our conﬁdence in the pointestimate is low because we are aware of issues in the underlying data: it isincomplete (e.g., Nigeria is missing) and some of the countries included do notseem to capture any farms smaller than 5 ha (e.g., Zambia).Notes1.
