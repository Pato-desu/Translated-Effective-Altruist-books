Last nontrivial update: 2019 Jul 18.SummaryThis piece critiques the article "Altruistic murders" by Ricardo Torres, which arguesagainst those interventions to reduce wild-animal suffering that require sacrificing someanimals for the sake of others. The central thrust of my reply is that we should try toactually help wild animals in the near term as effectively as possible, rather than toeing acautious ideological line to avoid offending the sensibilities of other animal advocates.ContentsSummaryIntroductionReplies to original articleConclusionReplies to follow-up comments, Apr. 2016Comments on John and Sebo (2019)IntroductionTorres's article is an eloquent expression of one set of views within the movement toreduce wild-animal suffering. For example, I've proposed covering lawns with gravel to reduceinvertebrate populations (). If you live in a cold enough climate thatmany bugs die over winter, then if you put the gravel on your yard right after the soilthaws in the spring, you may not kill that many existing animals. There will be someoverwintering bugs, as well as bug eggs that will hatch, and I suppose they might starveor otherwise die due to the gravel. But many of them would have died soon anyway. Giventhat most people, including many animal advocates, already routinely kill large numbersof invertebrates in their daily lives—even just when sitting on grass, hiking in the woods,or taking showers ()—I'm doubtful that the one-time, quasi-indirectbug deaths due to graveling a lawn would loom very large in terms of making people morecallous toward animals. Plus, graveling prevents ongoing, repeated harm to bugs frommowing the lawn, which is a violent process that kills or displaces numerous invertebrateseach time it occurs.
