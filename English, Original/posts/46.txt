Organisation-buildingBy Benjamin Todd · Published April 3rd, 2023
Many charities and social impact organisations probably don’t have much impact, but there are also many amazingorganisations out there — those focused on pressing problems, implementing an eﬀective and scalable solution, runby a great team, and in need of people. If you can ﬁnd a role that’s a good ﬁt at an organisation like this, it could wellbe one of the highest-impact things you can do.Most organisations also often need generalists able to do the bread and butter of building an organisation — hiringpeople, management, administration, communications, running software systems, crafting strategy, fundraising and soon.We call these ‘organisation building’ roles. They’re high-impact because you can increase the scale and eﬀectivenessof the organisation you’re working at, while also gaining skills that can be applied to a wide range of global problemsin the future (and make you generally employable too).Here, we’ll brieﬂy cover why this path is high-impact, what it involves, examples of people doing this work, how toenter and test out your ﬁt, and links to further resources and job opportunities.Why can organisation-building careers be high impact?First, you’ll develop a skillset that can be applied at most organisations. This means you’ll have opportunities to helptackle many diﬀerent global problems in the future. You’ll also be ﬂexibly able to work on many diﬀerent solutions toany given problem, if you ﬁnd better solutions later in your career. And you’ll be employable if you decide to earn togive or step back from doing good.Second, an employee who’s a good ﬁt can often be a signiﬁcant boost to an organisation, either by directly helpingthem deliver an impactful programme or by building the capacity of the organisation so that it can operate at agreater scale in the future. You could, for example, set up organisational infrastructure to enable the hiring of morepeople in the future, which increases the scale of the contribution towards solving pressing problems you can makewith your career.This is especially true for organisations that are more constrained by people with the right skills, rather than funding,which seems to be the case for many organisations within the problem areas we most recommend.What does building organisations involve?Organisation-building jobs are jobs in which you can:/one.tnum. Build generally useful organisational skills, such as operations, people management, fundraising, administration,software systems, ﬁnance etc/two.tnum. Then apply those skills to help build (or found) high-impact organisationsWhat skills are needed to build organisations?

Organisation builders typically have skills in areas like:Operations managementProject management (including setting objectives, metrics, etc.)People management and coaching (some manager jobs require specialised skills, but some just require generalmanagement-associated skills)Executive leadership (setting and achieving organisation-wide goals, making top-level decisions about budgeting,etc.)RecruitingFundraising and marketingHuman resourcesOﬃce managementEvents managementAssistant and administrative workCommunications and public relationsFinance and accountingCorporate & nonproﬁt lawMany organisations have a signiﬁcant need for generalists who span several of these areas. If your aim is to take aleadership position, it’s useful to have a shallow knowledge of most of them.You can also pick just one skill to specialise in — especially for areas like law and accounting that tend to be on theirown track.Generally larger organisations have a greater need for specialists, while those with under 50 employees hire moregeneralists.You can see a 2019 survey about which skills are most needed within the eﬀective altruism community.Also see our article on which skills make you most employable in the job market in general.Which organisations should I help build?Look for organisations that:/one.tnum. Are in need of your skillsThese organisations will most often be non-proﬁts, but they could also be research institutes, political organisationsor for-proﬁt companies with a social mission.For speciﬁc ideas, see our list of recommended organisations. You can also ﬁnd longer lists of suggestions withineach of our problem proﬁles.It can be even higher-impact to help found a new organisation rather than build an existing one. You frequently notice how things could be done more eﬃciently and have astrong internal drive to prevent avoidable errors and make things run more smoothly.You intuitively engage in systems thinking and enjoy going meta. This is a bit diﬃcult to summarise, but involvesthings like: you’d notice when people ask you similar questions multiple times, and then think about how toprevent the issue from coming up again. For example: “can you share me on this doc” turns into “what wentwrong such that this person didn’t already have access to everything they need, how can we improve namingconventions or sharing conventions in future?”You’re reliable, self-directed, able to manage your time well, and you can create eﬃcient and productive plansand keep track of complex projects.You might also be good at learning quickly and have high attention to detail.A 2019 survey of organisations in the eﬀective altruism community found that the most needed skills at eﬀectiveorganisations were generalist researchers, operations and management.Of course, diﬀerent role types will require diﬀerent skills. For example, being a COO or events manager requiresgreater social and system building skills — whereas working in ﬁnance requires fewer social skills, but moreconscientiousness and attention to detail.How to test your ﬁt over timeAll of these — individually or together — seem like good signs of being a strong ﬁt:You get job oﬀers (as contractors or staﬀ) at organisations you’d like to work for.You’re promoted within your ﬁrst two years.You receive excellent performance reviews.You’re asked to take on progressively more responsibility over time.Your manager / colleagues suggest you might take on more senior roles in future.You ask your superiors for their honest assessment of your ﬁt and they are positive (e.g. they tell you you’re in thetop 10% of people they can imagine doing your role).You’re able to multiply a superior’s time by over 2-20X, depending on the role type.If you’re aiming to build a new organisation, write out a list of ideas for new organisations you’d like to exist andget feedback from grantmakers and experts.If founding a new organisation, you get seed funding from a major grantmaker, like Open Philanthropy, LongviewPhilanthropy, EA Funds, or a private donor.How to get started in organisation-building careersYou can get started in any role that will let you start learning one of the skills listed above. Work in one specialisationwill often give you exposure to the others, and it’s often possible to move between them.There’s a good chance you won’t be able to work at one of our top recommended organisations right away for anumber of reasons:The organisations have limited mentorship capacity, so they most often hire people with a couple of years ofexperience rather than those fresh out of college (though there are exceptions).These organisations usually hire people who already have some expertise in the problem area they’re working on(e.g. AI safety, biosecurity), as these issues involve specialised knowledge.We chose our recommended problems in large part because they’re unusually neglected. But the fact thatthey’re neglected also means there aren’t many open positions or training programmes.If you think you might be able to get a job at one of our recommended organisations, we’d deﬁnitely encourage youto apply — even if you think it’s unlikely. We’ve advised many people who were underconﬁdent: they didn’t thinkthey’d get a particular job at a competitive organisation, then went on to land it, and they performed excellently. It’s
often worth the cost of applying, even if your chances are slim. Not only is it a good bet to take, but you’ll get valuableinformation about your ﬁt from doing so (see our guide to getting jobs). What’s crucial, however, is to have a back-upplan.You can develop the skills needed in this path in almost any organisation, so we’d encourage you to apply to bothsome ambitious stretch options, and some options you’re more conﬁdent of getting.It can be worth pursuing roles at organisations that don’t have much impact in order to build your skills, especially ifthose organisations are a good environment for learning or are otherwise gaining career capital.An especially good strategy to develop organisation-building skills early on is to work at any small-but-growingorganisations. In this sort of organisation, you often get a chance to try many diﬀerent things, and can get a richexposure to many facets of helping an organisation succeed. This can be an especially good way to get experiencewith people management and project management, which are often very generally applicable and in-demand skillsacross organisations. Coming into such a company in whatever role is available, and then being ﬂexible and simplyfocused on helping the company succeed, can be a good learning experience.One option within this category is to consider becoming an early employee at a tech startup. But any organisationthat’s high-performing can be a great place to develop your skills. One downside of this option, though, is that newercompanies may be more likely to fail, compared to more established employers.How to develop your career over timeOver time, you can narrow down which skills to focus on and look for jobs at organisations that are more and moreimpactful.People pursuing this type of career might sometimes have feelings like “I’m performing well, but I don’t feel I’mcontributing to a great mission.” In early career stages, it might be more important to perform well and build yourskills, setting yourself up to contribute much more with your career in the future, rather than being at an organisationwhose mission you’re enthusiastic about (assuming the work is overall reasonably enjoyable and sustainable). Then,later in your career — when you have a better sense of what you’re great at](/articles/personal-ﬁt/) and aren’t growingyour skills as rapidly — you’ll be able to add a lot of value to an organisation with that great mission.These same skills are also in-demand in the wider job market. That means that if you want to focus on personal goalsrather than impact for some part of your career, you’ll likely be a strong candidate for lots of jobs. Earning to give isanother back-up option.Find a job in this pathIf you think you might be a good ﬁt for this career, see our curated list of job opportunities for this path — you canﬁlter by ‘management’ and ‘operations’ to ﬁnd opportunities in this category.
